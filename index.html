  • Real-world data is exhaust from operations, not built for your question.
  • For each sheet: who fills it, when, and what one row is. Getting the grain right is half of analytics. Click “peek” to show real rows.
  • The interview form has the learner’s email, not their ID: matching email to learner is the first join, and a typo breaks it.

3 · Transform and store

Cleaning: before → after

Which topic is this question?

Programme

Checks before anyone sees a number

    Say (8 min):
    • Layers: raw is an exact copy (never edited); clean fixes formats and removes duplicates and personal data; the fact table is one row per question; the summary is what charts read. Click a step to open its table.
    • Every dropped row has a reason (the line under the pipeline). Trust = being able to answer “where did it go?”.
    • Before → after: ten spellings of a round become one; five date formats become one; free-text results become an outcome. Yellow = changed.
    • Topic box: learners’ own topic labels are useless (“misc”, “dsa”), so we read the question. Keyword rules first (cheap, explainable); the AI handles what they miss (about 85% right); a module owner corrects the AI. Try a clear question, then a vague one.
    • Checks: a failure stops the load and keeps yesterday’s numbers; a warning lets it through and tells someone. The warning is real: DevOps write-ups are three weeks late, so recent DevOps numbers look quieter than they were.

      5 · Read it

      Before acting, three questions to ask of any chart

      Missing answers

      What if every silent company had said yes, or no?

      Small samples

      How many answers before we trust a rate?

      Correlation

      Practice score vs clear rate: cause?

      Say (7 min):
      • Walkthrough (Next): axes, the corner, a red ring, a hollow bubble, LLM appearing.
      • Missing answers: about a fifth of questions have no verdict because the company went silent. We leave them out. Toggle “all cleared” / “all struggled”: bubbles shift, but the red-ringed topics stay worse than average either way. That is why the rule needs both ends.
      • Small samples: a topic with 5 answers can look terrible by chance. Below the minimum, bubbles go hollow and get no rating. At 5 we act on noise; at 50 we ignore real problems. Choosing a threshold is a judgement call: write it down. The 60-day window in chapter 4 makes most topics hollow.
      • Correlation: higher practice scores, higher clear rates. Does practice cause it, or do stronger learners practise more and get easier companies? And a third of interviews are never written up: if strugglers write up less, every rate is too rosy.
      6 · Decide

      One signal, one owner, one action, one measure

      How we’ll know it worked

      New module for the next cohort, old module for a matched cohort. Compare in three months.

      Say (5 min):
      • Analytics doesn’t decide; it hands a clear signal to the person who can act.
      • Curriculum: the shaded-corner topic in each programme goes to its module owner: rework the module, add a mock interview on it.
      • Partnerships: some problems aren’t ours to teach away. Companies that go silent get a feedback deadline or fewer learners.
      • Content: LLM is new or rising everywhere. Get ahead of it before it reaches the corner.
      • Measurement: agree the metric first; keep a comparison cohort (if the whole market gets easier, both improve and the difference is what the module did); add a guardrail so other topics don’t slip.
      • Ask: how did your three guesses do?
